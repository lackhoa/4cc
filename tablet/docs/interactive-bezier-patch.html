Coons patches from first principles

Three stages. Everything is draggable; in 3D views, drag empty space to orbit, wheel to zoom.

1 · The bezier curve (refresher)

4 control points, one parameter t ∈ [0,1]. The curve is repeated linear interpolation (de Casteljau): lerp neighbors → 3 points → lerp → 2 → lerp → 1 point on the curve. Expanding the lerps gives the Bernstein weights:

C(t) = (1−t)³·P₀ + 3(1−t)²t·P₁ + 3(1−t)t²·P₂ + t³·P₃
the bars show each point's Bernstein weight at this t — note only P₀/P₃ ever reach weight 1 (at the ends)

2 · Tensor product: a curve of curves

Add a second parameter v. Take 4 bezier curves running in the v direction (the columns of a 4×4 grid). At any v, evaluate all 4 columns — that gives 4 points — and use those as the control points of a bezier curve in u. Sweep v and the moving curve traces the surface:

S(u,v) = Σᵢ Σⱼ Bᵢ(u)·Bⱼ(v)·Pᵢⱼ

The "16 numbers" are the 16 grid points Pᵢⱼ: 4 corners (surface passes through), 8 edge points (shape the 4 boundary curves), 4 interior "belly" points (never touch the boundary).

White curve = the bezier-in-u whose 4 control points (small white dots) ride the column curves at the current v. Drag any point; click one to heatmap its influence Bᵢ(u)Bⱼ(v).

"How do you even 'add' two curves?" — is this legal math? (resolved side-question)

It's real, and it has a name: Coons' construction is a Boolean sum (Coons, late 1960s; Gordon generalized it — the field is called transfinite interpolation). "Adding sheets" means: each sheet is a function (s,t) → point in ℝ³, and you add them pointwise, coordinate by coordinate: (R1+R2)(s,t) = R1(s,t) + R2(s,t) as a plain vector sum. So the yellow sheet's left edge at parameter t is literally the point left(t) + chord(t) — coordinates summed. That's also why the yellow sheet floats way off: it sits at roughly double the distance from the origin.

And your suspicion is mathematically well-founded! Adding two positions is NOT a geometric operation by itself — the result depends on where you put the origin (the weights sum to 2, not 1). R1+R2 alone is meaningless scaffolding. Only the full combination R1 + R2 − B has weights 1 + 1 − 1 = 1 — an affine combination, origin-independent, hence a genuine geometric object. The subtraction isn't just fixing the edges; it's what makes the algebra geometrically legal at all.

Spoiler: the full construction (read after attempting Q2 / Q6)

Four boundary curves on a unit square: bottom(s), top(s) along s∈[0,1]; left(t), right(t) along t∈[0,1]. Corners P00 (bottom-left), P10, P11, P01. Three simple surfaces:

R1(s,t) = (1−t)·bottom(s) + t·top(s)

— correct on bottom and top, but its left edge is just the straight line P00→P01, ignoring the drawn left curve.

R2(s,t) = (1−s)·left(t) + s·right(t)

— correct on left and right, straight-line wrong on bottom and top.

B(s,t) = (1−s)(1−t)·P00 + s(1−t)·P10 + (1−s)t·P01 + st·P11

Coons: S = R1 + R2 − B. Along any edge, one ruled surface contributes the true curve, the other contributes the straight chord, and B equals exactly that chord — the chord cancels, so every edge is interpolated exactly. The interior is fully derived from the edges — no independent belly control (that's what a full bezier patch adds).

Worked check along the bottom edge (t=0):

S(s,0) = bottom(s) + [(1−s)·left(0) + s·right(0)] − [(1−s)·P00 + s·P10]

With exact corners (left(0)=P00, right(0)=P10) the brackets cancel → S(s,0) = bottom(s). Our corners are sketched, so coons.ts averages the two candidate endpoints into each corner and the leftover half-gap is linearly smeared along the rims instead of tearing — visible only when a side is dragged far from its neighbors.

Our implementation (tablet/src/coons.ts) — no Q2 spoilers
  • No bicubic fit: each side is presampled at 17 points (COONS_GRID=16 cells, sample_side, uniform over the stroke's bezier chain, world space) and the blend formula is evaluated directly on the 17×17 grid (surface_point).
  • Chaining (chain_sides): tap order and draw direction don't matter — greedy nearest-endpoint walk starting from the first tapped stroke orients the 4 strokes into a head-to-tail loop. Sides 2 and 3 of the loop run backwards relative to the (s,t) grid, hence the COONS_GRID − i indexing for top/left.
  • Linked, not baked: retessellated from current strokes every frame → editing any side reshapes the fill live.
  • Shading: per-face flat headlight like inflate (brightness from |normal·camera_forward|, ambient 0.35), baked into vertex colors.

Q8/Q11 ✓: R1 is a (3,1) patch whose 4×2 net is exactly b0..b3, t0..t3 — column i is the straight segment bᵢ→tᵢ, because factoring gives R1 = Σᵢ B³ᵢ(s)·[(1−t)·bᵢ + t·tᵢ]. You then asked "can we transform it into a (3,3) patch?" — that is exactly Q9:

Q9 ✓: the segment A→B as a cubic has controls at A, A+⅓(B−A), A+⅔(B−A), B. Why ⅓/⅔: as fractions along the segment the controls sit at 0, ⅓, ⅔, 1, and the Bernstein average 3(1−t)²t·⅓ + 3(1−t)t²·⅔ + t³ = t·[(1−t)+t]² = t — the thirds exactly cancel Bernstein's 3s, so the curve rides the line at speed t. Elevating all 4 columns turns R1's 4×2 net into 4×4: R1 as a (3,3) patch. Same trick elevates R2 (rows) and B (both directions). On to the finale:

Worked example: elevating B to (3,3) — two passes of the Q9 trick

B's own net is just the 2×2 corners. Elevate one direction at a time:

Pass 1 (s-direction): B's bottom row is the segment P00→P10, its top row P01→P11. Q9 each: the rows become 4 points at fractions 0, ⅓, ⅔, 1 along those segments. Now the net is 4×2.

Pass 2 (t-direction): each of the 4 columns of that 4×2 net is itself a straight segment (bottom-row point → top-row point). Q9 each again: 4 points per column at fractions 0, ⅓, ⅔, 1. Net is now 4×4.

Net effect: entry (i,j) sits at fraction i/3 across and j/3 up — which is precisely B evaluated at (s,t) = (i/3, j/3):

net(B)ᵢⱼ = B(i/3, j/3) = (1−i/3)(1−j/3)·P00 + (i/3)(1−j/3)·P10 + (1−i/3)(j/3)·P01 + (i/3)(j/3)·P11

(That "net = surface sampled at the grid fractions" shortcut works for B because B is linear in both directions. For R1 only the t-direction is linear, so elevation gives net(R1)ᵢⱼ = (1−j/3)·bᵢ + (j/3)·tᵢ — the lerp applied to control points bᵢ, tᵢ, not to points on the curves. Mirror for R2: net(R2)ᵢⱼ = (1−i/3)·lⱼ + (i/3)·rⱼ.)

All three nets now have the same degree (3,3), so they add entry-by-entry. The full sum, ready to manipulate (this IS the answer's raw form — Q10 is to simplify/recognize it):

Cᵢⱼ = (1−j/3)·bᵢ + (j/3)·tᵢ
     + (1−i/3)·lⱼ + (i/3)·rⱼ
     − [(1−i/3)(1−j/3)·P00 + (i/3)(1−j/3)·P10 + (1−i/3)(j/3)·P01 + (i/3)(j/3)·P11]

Sanity workout before the belly: try a boundary entry, e.g. i=0 (left column, remember b0=l0=P00, t0=l3=P01) — the entry should collapse to just lⱼ. Then do C11 (i=1, j=1).

Khoa's observation: i and j never appear alone — always as thirds. So substitute u = i/3, v = j/3:

Cᵢⱼ = (1−v)·bᵢ + v·tᵢ + (1−u)·lⱼ + u·rⱼ − [(1−u)(1−v)·P00 + u(1−v)·P10 + (1−u)v·P01 + uv·P11]

Now compare, line by line, with the surface formula from stage 3's spoiler:

S(s,t) = (1−t)·bottom(s) + t·top(s) + (1−s)·left(t) + s·right(t) − [(1−s)(1−t)·P00 + s(1−t)·P10 + (1−s)t·P01 + st·P11]

Same shape. What's playing the role of bottom(s), top(s), left(t), right(t)? Say it out loud — that's the Eureka, and the rest of Q10 (C11 concretely) is plugging in u = v = ⅓.

The interior-point formula (derived 2026-08-31 — spoils Q5(c))

Sourcing note: derived by degree elevation (below), edge-checked, and it matches the implicit-control-point formula of PDF Type 6 (Coons mesh) shadings; no freely published statement found, so treat this algebra as primary and verify numerically during parity work.

Boundary control points: bottom b0..b3, top t0..t3 (both running in s); left l0..l3, right r0..r3 (both in t). Corners shared: b0=l0=P00, b3=r0=P10, t0=l3=P01, t3=r3=P11.

Derivation: each sheet is already a low-degree bezier patch — R1 is degree (3,1), R2 is (1,3), B is (1,1). Degree-elevate all three to (3,3) and add the control nets pointwise:

net(S)ᵢⱼ = (1−j/3)·bᵢ + (j/3)·tᵢ + (1−i/3)·lⱼ + (i/3)·rⱼ − bilinear(i/3, j/3; corners)

The elegant bit: the control net of the Coons patch is the Coons construction applied to the control points themselves, sampled at (i/3, j/3). Boundary rows/columns collapse to the original boundary control points (same cancellation as the surface-level edge check). The 4 interior points written out:

C11 = 2/3(b1+l1) + 1/3(t1+r1) − (4·P00 + 2·P10 + 2·P01 + 1·P11)/9
C21 = 2/3(b2+r1) + 1/3(t2+l1) − (2·P00 + 4·P10 + 1·P01 + 2·P11)/9
C12 = 2/3(t1+l2) + 1/3(b1+r2) − (2·P00 + 1·P10 + 4·P01 + 2·P11)/9
C22 = 2/3(t2+r2) + 1/3(b2+l2) − (1·P00 + 2·P10 + 2·P01 + 4·P11)/9

(Each is: 2/3 of the two near edge tangential points + 1/3 of the two far ones, minus the corner sheet at that grid position.)

Hermite note: the bicubically-blended (Hermite) Coons variant is also a bicubic bezier patch per cell — different interior points (they encode the prescribed cross-derivatives) but the same target format. So a later C1 upgrade changes only the interior-point computation, not the parity format.

Chains → patch grid (the honest complication): our sides are bezier chains, so one fill is an N×M grid of bicubic patches — exact per-cell only if opposite sides share knot positions in the blend parameter. Parity conversion = union of s-knots (bottom+top) × union of t-knots (left+right), split all four chains there (de Casteljau subdivision), then the formula per cell. Wrinkle: sample_side parameterizes uniformly over the chain parameter, which the desktop conversion must reproduce — or both move to arc-length; pick one before parity.

Sourcing: Coons over composite/spline boundaries is standard CAGD (the construction is transfinite — any parametric boundaries); PDF Type 6 shadings are deployed meshes of bezier-boundary Coons patches; de Casteljau subdivision and knot-union compatibility are textbook. The assembled recipe above is our composition of those pieces, not one published algorithm — hence the numeric verification step.

C1 stitching reality check ("would it really work?")

Short answer: not with our variant, no. Two bilinearly blended Coons patches sharing a boundary curve are C0 for free (same edge curve on both sides), but the cross-boundary derivative of each patch is derived — determined by that patch's opposite side and corners — so the two derivatives generally disagree and you get a visible crease. The linear blend has no degree of freedom to prescribe them.

What "C1 Coons" actually requires, in increasing order of pain:

  1. Hermite (bicubically blended) Coons: the blend takes not just the 4 edge curves but also a cross-derivative field along each edge, using cubic Hermite weights instead of (1−t, t). Give both patches the same prescribed cross-derivative along the shared edge → C1.
  2. Twist compatibility at corners: where 4 patches meet, the prescribed derivative fields must agree on the mixed second derivative (the "twist vector"). Incompatible twists are the classic failure; Gregory patches exist to paper over it (at the cost of rational, messier surfaces).
  3. Sketch tools usually cheat: estimate a shared normal/derivative field by averaging the two patches' natural derivatives along the edge → approximate G1, good enough visually.

Design consequence: creases at patch borders may actually be fine (drawn edges often ARE creases — a box wants them). Smooth multi-patch blobs are better served by inflate; if we ever need smooth patch networks, that's the Hermite upgrade + averaged derivative field (option 3), not the bilinear blend we have.
