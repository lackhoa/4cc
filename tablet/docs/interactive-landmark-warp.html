How a landmark warp moves the head

Fitting the template to a target means moving hundreds of control points. You place about 20 landmarks. The warp decides where every other point goes. Below is the same thing in 2D, on a profile, with 10 landmarks.

1 · Try it

Each landmark is a pair. The hollow ring is where it sits on the template. The yellow dot is where it must end up on the target. Drag a yellow dot. The skin, the skull and the grid all follow.

2 · What the warp is

The warp is a rule for points: give it any point, it returns the moved point. It knows nothing about curves, skin or bone. The grid shows the rule itself: every grid crossing is a point fed through it.

A curve moves because each of its control points is fed through the rule. That is the answer to "the skull moves how?". No landmark sits on the skull. The skull points are just points in the same space, so they ride along with the space around them. Drag the nose tip landmark and watch the nasal bone: it follows, but less, because it is further from the landmark.

Smooth means the grid bends but never tears or folds. Two points that start close end close. So a curve stays a curve, and the skull stays inside the skin.

3 · The rule has two parts

Press this button, then switch between the two modes in section 1.

Affine part. Affine = a linear transform plus a shift. A linear transform alone keeps the origin fixed, so it cannot slide the head. One scale, stretch, shear and shift for the whole head, chosen so the landmarks get as close to their targets as one such move allows. The grid stays straight. The landmarks do not land exactly: red lines show how far each one misses.

Local part. Whatever is left. Each landmark pulls the space around it the rest of the way to its target. The pull is strongest at the landmark and fades with distance. Now every landmark lands exactly, and the grid bends.

Why the teal probe point is here. It is only a teaching aid on this page. The app has no probe. The warp is a rule for points, and the probe is one point that you feed through the rule by hand, so you can ask "where does a point here go, and why?". The curves and the grid show only where points end up. The probe also shows how the move is made up: the grey arrow is its affine move, the pink arrow is its local move.

Drag it anywhere. Put it on a landmark ring: the two arrows together end exactly on the yellow dot. Put it between two landmarks: it gets a blend. Sections 4 and 5 show the numbers behind both arrows.

4 · Computing the affine part

The affine part is six numbers: a, b, c, d, e, f. They move any point (x, y) like this:

new x = a + b·x + c·y
new y = d + e·x + f·y

With the landmarks as they are now in section 1, the six numbers are:

How the six numbers are chosen. It is the trend line of a spreadsheet, with two inputs in place of one. Take any six numbers. Feed each landmark's template position through them. The result misses that landmark's target by some distance. Square each miss and add them all up. That sum is the score. Lower is better.

No search is needed to find the lowest score. Each miss is the six numbers times known values, added up. So the score is a bowl with one lowest point. At the bottom of a bowl the slope is zero in every direction.

See the score and the bowl. The top picture shows the head moved by the affine part alone. The white dot is where a landmark lands, the yellow dot is its target, the red line is the miss. On each miss stands a red square with the miss as its side, so its area is the squared miss. The score is the total red area.

Now pick one of the six numbers and drag the slider to push it away from its best value. The head shifts or stretches, the squares grow, the score goes up. The bottom picture plots the score against that number: a parabola with one bottom. The computed value sits exactly at the bottom. That holds for all six numbers at once, which is what "bottom of the bowl" means.

The score splits into two separate problems. A squared miss distance is (miss in x)² + (miss in y)². The miss in x is a + b·xᵢ + c·yᵢ − Xᵢ: it contains only a, b, c and the target x. The miss in y is d + e·xᵢ + f·yᵢ − Yᵢ: it contains only d, e, f and the target y. So the score is one sum that only a, b, c can change, plus one sum that only d, e, f can change. Each half is minimized on its own. Changing a never moves a point up or down, so the target y cannot appear in the equations for a, b, c.

Writing "slope is zero" for a, for b and for c gives three plain linear equations. They hold only the target x:

sum over landmarks of      (a + b·xᵢ + c·yᵢ − Xᵢ) = 0
sum over landmarks of xᵢ · (a + b·xᵢ + c·yᵢ − Xᵢ) = 0
sum over landmarks of yᵢ · (a + b·xᵢ + c·yᵢ − Xᵢ) = 0

(xᵢ, yᵢ) is where landmark i sits on the template. Xᵢ is its target x. Everything except a, b, c is a known number. Three equations, three unknowns, solved by elimination as in school.

The other half gives the same three equations with d, e, f in place of a, b, c and the target y, Yᵢ, in place of Xᵢ:

sum over landmarks of      (d + e·xᵢ + f·yᵢ − Yᵢ) = 0
sum over landmarks of xᵢ · (d + e·xᵢ + f·yᵢ − Yᵢ) = 0
sum over landmarks of yᵢ · (d + e·xᵢ + f·yᵢ − Yᵢ) = 0

Note that the template position (xᵢ, yᵢ) appears in both sets. Only the target is split.

What "leftover" means. The affine part is one rule for the whole head, with only six numbers. It cannot put all 10 landmarks exactly on their targets. Each landmark ends up a little off. The leftover of a landmark is the step still missing: from where the affine part put it, to where it must be.

leftover = target − affine result

Example, the nose tip with the teenage girl landmarks. Its target is (198.0, 284.0). The affine part puts it at (188.5, 279.4). So its leftover is (9.5, 4.6): it still has to go 9.5 px right and 4.6 px down. A leftover of (0, 0) would mean the affine part already hit the target.

The leftovers are the red lines in "affine part only" mode in section 1. They are also the whole input of the local part: its only job is to move each landmark by its leftover.

5 · Computing the local part

The job: find a rule that moves each landmark by exactly its leftover, and moves the points in between smoothly.

The local part answers one question: given a point p on the template, how far does it move? p can be any point: a control point of a curve, or the teal probe in section 1. The answer is built from three things.

1. r, a distance. r is the straight-line distance between p and one landmark, measured with a ruler on the template. It is one plain number. Example: p is at (340, 260) and the landmark sits at (300, 230) on the template. They are 40 apart in x and 30 apart in y, so r = √(40² + 30²) = 50. There is one r per landmark: r₁ is the distance from p to landmark 1, r₂ from p to landmark 2, and so on. With 10 landmarks, p has 10 distances. The targets play no part in r.

2. U, a fixed formula. U turns a distance into another number: U(r) = r² · ln r. It is the same formula for every landmark and it has nothing to solve for. Why this formula is explained at the end of the section.

3. w, a weight per landmark. Each landmark i has a weight wᵢ. A weight is a small arrow: an x amount and a y amount. The weights are the only unknowns. How they are found comes next.

Putting them together. For each landmark, scale its arrow wᵢ by the number U(rᵢ). Add up the scaled arrows. That sum, plus a correction, is how far p moves:

local(p) = w₁·U(r₁) + w₂·U(r₂) + … + wₙ·U(rₙ) + correction(p)

The table "One point, worked out" below shows exactly this sum for the probe: one row per landmark with its r, its U(r), its weight and the scaled arrow.

The correction is a second affine move. Written out, with p = (x, y):

correction in x = a′ + b′·x + c′·y
correction in y = d′ + e′·x + f′·y

That is the same kind of formula as the affine part in section 4, with six numbers of its own, marked with ′ to keep them apart: a fixed shift (a′ and d′) plus an amount that grows evenly with x and with y. No U, no distances, no landmarks in it. In the sheet picture it is a flat board, shifted and tilted, and the sheet's bumps sit on top of it. Its six numbers are unknowns, solved together with the weights. The reason it is needed comes below.

Finding the weights. The weights are the unknowns. What we know: at each landmark, the local move must equal that landmark's leftover. That is one equation per landmark:

for landmark j:   w₁·U(distance j to 1) + … + wₙ·U(distance j to n) + correction(j) = leftover of j

Every U(...) here is a known number, because the template positions are known. So this is again a set of linear equations. Three more equations go with the three numbers of the correction:

sum of wᵢ = 0     sum of wᵢ·xᵢ = 0     sum of wᵢ·yᵢ = 0

Why a steel sheet at all. The problem to solve: the moves are known at 10 landmarks, and a move is needed for every other point. Infinitely many rules agree at the 10 landmarks. Something must pick one. The wish is "as smooth as possible in between", but that is not yet a rule a computer can follow. Three simple attempts and what goes wrong:

  • Copy the move of the nearest landmark. The drawing tears on the line halfway between two landmarks.
  • Average the landmark moves, nearer ones count more. A move no landmark asks for can never come out. If all landmarks say "scale by 1.2", points outside them should move further than any landmark, but an average cannot exceed its inputs.
  • A round bump around each landmark. The bump needs a width, and nothing says what it should be. Too narrow gives dents, too wide smears everything.

The steel sheet is a fourth rule, and it makes "smooth" exact. A springy sheet forced through given heights settles into the shape with the least total bending. Nature solves "smoothest surface through these points" for free. So the method says: use that shape. It has no width to choose, it does not tear, and a move that needs no bending at all, any affine move, costs nothing and comes out exactly. The rest of this section is only how to compute that shape.

What "total bending" means, exactly. Call the sheet's height h(x, y). Its slope in x is how fast h changes as x grows. Bending is how fast the slope changes. There are three such rates at every spot:

  • h_xx: how fast the slope in x changes as x grows.
  • h_yy: the same for y.
  • h_xy: how fast the slope in x changes as y grows. This is twist.
total bending = sum over the whole plane of ( h_xx² + 2·h_xy² + h_yy² )

Three examples:

h(x, y)h_xxh_xyh_yybending at each spot
5 + 2·x − 3·y, a tilted flat board0000

"Bends as little as possible" means: of all the sheets that pass through every peg, take the one whose total bending is the smallest number. That is a precise mathematical problem, and its answer is proven to be exactly the formula of this section: weights times U(r), plus a correction, with the three extra equations. Squaring makes one sharp crease cost more than the same turn spread out, so the winner spreads its turning widely.

What is exact and what is only a picture. The definition above and the formula are exact. The steel, the fingers and the "force" are a way to picture them. Real thin plates do follow this rule for small bending, but nothing in the warp depends on that.

Where "force" comes from. The sheet is thin springy steel. That is the "thin plate" in the name. The picture is this:

  • Lay a flat steel sheet over the template. Look only at the x part of the local move for now.
  • Let the height of the sheet above a point stand for how far that point moves in x. Flat sheet at height 0 means nothing moves.
  • Each landmark must move by its leftover. So at each landmark the sheet must be at a given height. Example: leftover in x of +4 px means the sheet is 4 high there.
  • To get the sheet to those heights, press on it at the landmarks with a finger, up at some and down at others. Between the landmarks the steel bends smoothly by itself. That smooth shape is the local move of every other point.

The steel itself does not stretch or expand in this picture. It only bends up and down, like a ruler pressed in the middle. The height is a stand-in: it is not a real distance above the drawing, it is the number "how far this point moves in x". The drawing does stretch, and the slope of the sheet says where. Example: height 0 at x = 100 and height 4 at x = 110. The first point stays, the second moves 4 px right, so the 10 px between them become 14 px. Where the sheet is level, even if high, the drawing only slides and keeps its size.

Now the link to the formula. Physics gives the shape of a large thin sheet pressed at one single point: its height at distance r from the finger is the pressing strength times r²·ln r. That is U(r). So U was not picked by taste. And the weight wᵢ is how hard the finger presses at landmark i, with the sign for up or down. That pressing strength is the "force". Several fingers add up, which is the sum of wᵢ·U(rᵢ). A second sheet does the same for the y part, with its own weights.

A bump that fades with distance is what one expects. U is not that. U alone is never used as a result. It is one ingredient, and the clamped plate below shows what it looks like once the other ingredients are added.

What U is, exactly. U is a function of one number. A distance goes in, a number comes out: U(r) = r²·ln r. Nothing else. It has no landmarks and no leftovers in it. It is the fixed building block. Each landmark contributes one copy of it, centred on that landmark and multiplied by that landmark's weight. All the fitting is in the weights.

Is it real physics? Yes. It is the textbook theory of thin plates for small bending (Timoshenko, "Theory of Plates and Shells"). The case that looks the way one expects is a round plate clamped at its rim, radius a, pushed at the centre. Its height at distance r from the centre is

height = strength × ( r²·ln(r / a) + (a² − r²) / 2 )

In the warp nothing is steel. The physics is only where the formula comes from. What holds our sheet is not a rim but the other landmarks and the correction, and the equations work out how.

What the logarithm is doing there. It is not a choice. It falls out of two demands: the sheet bends as little as possible, and the sheet is a 2D surface. "Bends as little as possible, pressed at one point only" allows just four round shapes in 2D:

  • a constant. A flat sheet. No finger at all.
  • r². A smooth bowl. Also no finger. It is bent by its edges.
  • ln r. Minus infinity at the finger. A hole, not a sheet.
  • r²·ln r. Finite at the finger, and pressed exactly there. The only one of the four that is a finger on a sheet.

The same demand in other dimensions gives other formulas, with no logarithm:

what bendsU(r)
a wire (1D)r³
a sheet (2D)r²·ln r
a block of space (3D)r

The wire row is the ordinary cubic spline: pieces of degree 3 joined smoothly. The block row is what the app will use, since its warp is 3D. So the logarithm belongs to this 2D page only.

Can U be replaced? Yes. The equations work with many other U, and the landmarks still land exactly on their targets. Only the behaviour between and beyond the landmarks changes:

  • r or r³ in 2D. Works. No longer the least bending shape. r makes a small sharp point at each landmark.
  • a round bump, exp(−(r / width)²). Works, and it fades with distance, which is the shape one expects. But the width has to be picked by hand. Too narrow and each landmark moves only its own spot. Too wide and the equations become hard to solve accurately.

r²·ln r is used here because it needs no width and gives the least bending.

The fading comes from adding several of them. Forces that cancel, which is what the three equations below demand, make the growing parts cancel too. The table further down shows it: one finger gives 6,907,755 at 1000 px, three balanced fingers give 1,679.

A sheet that is pressed but held by nothing does two things before it bends: it flies off if the forces do not cancel, and it spins if they are off balance. Only forces that cancel both ways leave pure bending. The three equations say exactly that:

  • sum of wᵢ = 0 means no net push. The forces add up to zero. Example: +3 at one landmark and −3 at another. If they did not add up to zero, the whole sheet would be pushed one way.
  • sum of wᵢ·xᵢ = 0 and sum of wᵢ·yᵢ = 0 mean no net tilt. The first equation alone does not give this. Example: +1 at x = 0 and −1 at x = 10. The sum of wᵢ is 0, but the sum of wᵢ·xᵢ is −10. One end is pressed up and the other down, so the sheet turns like a seesaw. The two extra equations forbid that.

See it. The picture is the sheet seen from the side, cut along x. The grey line is the flat sheet. The orange line is the sum of wᵢ·U(rᵢ). Each arrow is one finger: up for a positive weight, down for a negative weight, longer for −2. Each case is stretched to fill the picture, so compare the shapes here and the sizes in the readout.

  • One finger. Both sides go up without limit. That is the net push.
  • Two fingers. The left side goes down and the right side goes up, and both keep going. That is the seesaw, the net tilt.
  • Three fingers. Push and tilt both cancel. What is left is a dent at the fingers and a sheet that rises only very slowly far away. The readout shows how small it is next to the other two.

With a push or a tilt left in, the local part grows without limit far from the landmarks, faster than any affine move. With all three equations the far terms cancel almost completely. There is a second reason. Pushing or tilting the whole sheet is exactly what an affine move does. Without the three equations the same move could be written either in the weights or in the correction, and the solution would not be unique. With n landmarks that is n + 3 equations and n + 3 unknowns. Here n = 10, so 13 equations, solved by the same elimination.

Look at which rows are big. The far landmarks have the biggest rows, because U grows with distance. The correction row is big too, in the opposite direction. They cancel, and the small local move is what remains.

Why this odd U. U alone is not a bump. It is zero at its own landmark and it grows with distance. Only the weighted sum behaves, because the three extra equations and the correction make the growth cancel. U is the shape a thin metal plate takes when it is pushed at one point. The sum is a plate pushed at every landmark: it passes through all of them with the least total bending. That is the name: thin-plate spline.

An easier picture with the same steps: swap U for a real bump, exp(−r²/s²). Then each landmark has a bump and the weights come from the same equations. The cost is one more choice, the bump width s. The plate function needs no width. In 3D the plate function is U(r) = r and nothing else changes.

6 · What this means for the plan

Only the skin can be checked. Loomis drew skin, so landmarks go on skin features. The fitted skull is what the warp carries along. Where the skin is stretched, the tissue between skin and bone is stretched by the same amount. That is a guess, not anatomy.

More landmarks, tighter fit. Far from every landmark nothing pins the warp, so it only continues what the nearest landmarks do. A region that looks wrong after the warp needs one more landmark there, or a hand fix.

The app does this in 3D. Same rule, with x, y and z. A landmark seen in the front view gives x and y. The same landmark seen in the profile gives y and z.

Method: thin-plate spline. Affine part = least-squares affine fit. Local part = the spline minus that fit.
