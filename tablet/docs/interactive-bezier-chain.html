Patches over bezier chains

Spun off from interactive-bezier-patch.html. Q10 converts a Coons patch with single-cubic sides into one bicubic patch — but real sides are chains (tablet/src/document.ts: beziers: CubicBezier2[]). This page is about making the conversion work anyway: what s ∈ [0,1] means across a chain, and how a fill over chains becomes a grid of bicubic patches. Question numbers continue the patch file's numbering (Q13/Q14 moved here).

1 · Two chains and their rulings

A bottom and a top chain, 3 cubic segments each (, squares are the joints, everything draggable). Rendering the two curves never needs a global parameter — each segment draws itself. The patch does need one: the Coons blend pairs bottom(s) with top(s) at the same s — every ruling is such a pair. So a rule for spreading s ∈ [0,1] across the chain decides which point marries which. Two candidate rules shown at once: uniform (each segment gets an equal ⅓ of s — exactly what sample_side in coons.ts does) and arc-length (equal s steps travel equal distances).

The bottom chain's segments run short→long; the top's run long→short — on purpose. Toggle the two ruling families: identical four edges, yet the two families sweep different surfaces. Drag joints around to exaggerate or kill the difference.

2 · "Split at the union of break-values", illustrated

Concrete example: bottom is a chain of 2 cubics — its break sits at s = ½. Top is a chain of 3 — breaks at s = ⅓ and ⅔. (Break positions are parameter values under the chosen rule from Q14. Under the uniform rule they're these exact fractions by definition — joint k of an N-chain is at s = k/N, geometry never consulted. Under the arc-length rule the same joints would land at data-dependent fractions of total length — the construction still works, the numbers are just uglier.) Squares are breaks a side already has; circles are the new cuts we make with the Q13 split operation, so that afterwards both sides break at the same set {⅓, ½, ⅔}:

Why cut at exactly these values? They're where the surface changes formula. The Coons blend reads bottom(s) and top(s); when s crosses a joint of either chain, one of those switches to a different cubic — so the surface is piecewise-polynomial and its seams are the vertical lines at (bottom's breaks ∪ top's breaks). A bicubic patch is one polynomial, so it can only match the surface on a cell that straddles no seam. The union is the minimal cut set achieving that; the strip s ∈ [⅓, ½] above is seam-free, while e.g. [0.25, 0.6] would contain two seams — no single bicubic can follow a kink in the formula.

Then play the same game in the other direction: union the break-values of left and right to cut horizontal rows. Columns × rows = the grid of cells. Each cell's bottom/top pieces come from the cuts above; each cell's left/right pieces are iso-curves of the surface (which turn out to be single cubics in t already). Q13's remaining question is whether running Q10's conversion on each cell reproduces the big surface exactly.

Fact (relevant to Q14): our joints are G1, not C1 — the parameter speed jumps

When the fitter (tablet/src/fit.ts, Schneider's "FitCurve") splits a stroke, both halves share the split point and one tangent direction (fit.ts:103 computes a single tangent_center, negated for the second half) — but each half's least-squares solve picks its own handle length. So joints are G1: the curve looks smooth, yet the parametric "speed" |dC/dt| jumps across the joint. Invisible to the eye, very visible to anything that consumes the parameter — the kink/G1/C1 badges and the two smoothing buttons in the playground demonstrate it.

4 · Resolved

  • Q13 (first half) the split operation → blossoming / de Casteljau subdivision (2026-08-31). Khoa recalled blossoming — correct, and it is the Stage-1 scaffolding in disguise: the de Casteljau triangle at s IS a blossom evaluation, and its two flanks (first point of each row, last point of each row) are the control points of the two halves. In polar-form notation the left half's controls are b(0,0,0), b(0,0,s), b(0,s,s), b(s,s,s). The split is exact — the two cubics jointly trace the original curve. (Sourcing: blossoming is Ramshaw 1987 / de Boor's polar forms — textbook, not invented here.) Remaining half of Q13 above.
  • Q13 (second half) grid exactness → parked; decision: no re-encoding (2026-08-31). "Why do we care about the reencoding?" — the tablet never needs it (it samples S = R1 + R2 − B directly); it would matter only for a consumer whose primitive is the bicubic net (desktop Primitive_Type_Patch, PDF Type 6/7). Khoa's call: "we'll just add the functionality to the desktop" — port Coons-over-chains evaluation (chain_sides + sample_side + the blend) natively instead of converting. That gives exact tablet/desktop agreement by construction, at the cost of a new variable-length primitive there rather than reusing the existing patch machinery. The Q14 lesson is the one carry-over: the ported code must share the identical uniform s-rule (copy the code, don't re-derive). The grid-exactness proof stays parked in section 3 in case a fixed-format export ever becomes real.
  • Q14 the chain parameter → resolved (2026-08-31). First objection: "you just render the curves as separate curves" — true for drawing the boundary, but the patch's rulings pair bottom(s) with top(s), so a global s-rule decides which point marries which. Khoa's read of the code: sample_side is "the simple version that doesn't care about length" — uniform (equal s share per segment, geometry never consulted). Consequence: anything else that re-evaluates the same strokes must use the same rule, or the surfaces silently differ in the interior despite identical edges. (The rules coincide when segments have equal arc length — drag the playground into that state to see the two families merge.)
